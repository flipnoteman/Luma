The Luma Guide

Vision: Canny edges, anisotropic diffusion, mean shift

Three image operations of ArrayFire’s image.h, built-in kernels that run on every backend and follow ArrayFire’s CUDA kernels step by step (its CPU backend disagrees with its CUDA backend in several places; where it does, luma does what the CUDA backend does). All three take [H, W] images (axis 0, the height, varies fastest), batch over axes 2 and 3 (mean shift of a colour image takes [H, W, 3] and batches over axis 3), are lazy, record no synchronisation and poison their result on an error.

canny

use luma::{Array, CannyThreshold};

// A soft vertical edge near column 9 (a perfectly symmetric profile would tie between two
// columns and have no strict maximum).
let data: Vec<f32> = (0..400).map(|k| 100.0 * (1.0 + (((k / 20) as f32 - 9.2) / 1.5).tanh())).collect();
let img = Array::from_slice(&data, [20, 20])?;
let edges = img.canny(CannyThreshold::Manual, 0.2, 0.5, 3, false).to_vec()?;
assert!(edges[10 + 20 * 9] && !edges[10 + 20 * 5]);
Ok::<(), luma::LumaError>(())

canny(threshold, low, high, sobel_window, fast) -> Array<bool> runs ArrayFire’s pipeline:

  1. a 5-tap Gaussian ([0.11021, 0.23691, 0.30576, 0.23691, 0.11021], zero outside the image) in both directions, then the 3 x 3 Sobel derivatives of the result (the border mirrored without repeating the edge pixel, ArrayFire’s reflect101) and the magnitude sqrt(gx² + gy²), or |gx| + |gy| with fast;
  2. non-maximal suppression of the interior pixels: a pixel keeps its magnitude if it is strictly larger than the two neighbours along the gradient direction, which fall between pixels and are linearly interpolated; everything else (and the outermost ring) becomes 0;
  3. thresholds. With CannyThreshold::Manual low and high are ratios of the largest suppressed magnitude of the whole array (all planes of a batch share it). With CannyThreshold::AutoOtsu the high threshold comes from Otsu’s method on the histogram of the suppressed magnitudes of each plane (floor(max) bins between 0 and the array’s maximum, the zeros counted, the first maximum of the between-class variance; at most 16384 bins) and low is the ratio of the high threshold the low one has; high is ignored. The bin count follows floor(max), so the result depends on the scale of the image like in ArrayFire: use images in 0..255;
  4. hysteresis: pixels at or above the high threshold are edges, and so are the pixels at or above the low threshold that are 8-connected to an edge through such pixels.

Only sobel_window == 3 exists (ArrayFire rejects every other value as well).

The hysteresis on the GPU. Edge pixels are rare, so the work is organised around them. A first kernel finds the connected components of the candidates inside every 32 x 8 tile in workgroup memory and records which of them contain a strong pixel; a second links the components of neighbouring tiles with a lock-free union-find (the larger root goes under the smaller one); a third gives the final roots the strong flag and the last one writes the packed bits of the result. The last three visit one invocation per 32 pixels and skip the empty words, so their cost follows the number of edge pixels, and the whole detector is 6 launches (7 with Otsu) with nothing read back. ArrayFire needs about twenty kernels and a host round trip per hysteresis sweep.

Differences from ArrayFire: its CPU backend marks the whole outer ring and mixes up the axes of images that are not square (luma follows the CUDA backend: no ring, any shape). ArrayFire needs images of at least 5 x 5; luma accepts any shape (an image without an interior pixel has no edges). A constant image makes ArrayFire fail; luma gives the edges near the border that the zero padding of the smoothing creates. Pixels whose suppressed magnitudes tie in exact arithmetic (the sides of a perfectly flat square, say) can fall on either side in f32; luma mirrors the order of ArrayFire’s operations to land on the same side in most cases, but not all. The same goes for Otsu thresholds when several bins tie (empty bins between the classes): ArrayFire’s rounding picks one, luma the lowest, which moves the weak threshold slightly.

anisotropic_diffusion

use luma::{Array, DiffusionEq, FluxFunction};

let mut data = vec![0.0f32; 16 * 16];
data[8 + 16 * 8] = 80.0;
let img = Array::from_slice(&data, [16, 16])?;
let out = img.anisotropic_diffusion(0.1, 5.0, 3, FluxFunction::Exponential, DiffusionEq::Gradient).to_vec()?;
assert!(out[8 + 16 * 8] < 80.0 && out[7 + 16 * 8] > 0.0);
Ok::<(), luma::LumaError>(())

anisotropic_diffusion(timestep, conductance, iterations, flux, equation) runs iterations steps of Perona-Malik diffusion (DiffusionEq::Gradient) or modified curvature diffusion (DiffusionEq::Mcde, which always uses the exponential flux). The conductance K is relative: every step the exponent factor is -1 / (2 K² mean(g²)) with g the gradient of the current image and the mean over every element of the array. The flux through a pixel face is c(x) * (difference) with c = exp(x) (FluxFunction::Exponential) or c = 1 / (1 + x) (FluxFunction::Quadratic) of x = factor * |grad u|² at the face; outside the image the nearest pixel is used.

ArrayFire’s quadratic function has the sign of x inverted (the factor is negative), which makes it exceed 1 and blow up where the gradient is large; luma reproduces it so that the results are the same: prefer the exponential flux. There is no stability check of the time step (it is stable up to about 0.125), as in ArrayFire, which rejects only iterations == 0 and a conductance of 0 (luma too). A constant image comes back unchanged (ArrayFire’s is NaN).

Every step is three launches: the gradient energy per tile, one workgroup that adds the tiles up and writes the factor (ArrayFire reads the sum back to the host after every step), and the update from one image into the other. luma reads and writes different images, so the result does not depend on the order of the pixels; ArrayFire’s CPU backend updates in place pixel by pixel. ArrayFire’s CUDA kernel has a bug: on images with more than about 70 columns it updates only the leading columns along axis 1 (72 of 128, 96 of 300 in the sizes probed) and leaves the rest unchanged. luma updates every pixel. On the columns ArrayFire does update one step agrees with it to f32 rounding on small images; on large ones its in-place update also races between tiles and varies from run to run.

mean_shift

use luma::Array;

let mut data = vec![10.0f32; 8 * 8];
for j in 4..8 { for i in 0..8 { data[i + 8 * j] = 200.0; } }
let img = Array::from_slice(&data, [8, 8])?;
let out = img.mean_shift(1.0, 20.0, 2, false).to_vec()?;
assert_eq!((out[0], out[7 + 8 * 7]), (10.0, 200.0));
Ok::<(), luma::LumaError>(())

mean_shift(spatial_sigma, chromatic_sigma, iterations, is_color) starts a walk at every pixel. The window is 2 r + 1 pixels a side with r = max(floor(1.5 * spatial_sigma), 1), cut at the image. A step collects the window pixels whose squared colour distance (summed over the channels, [H, W, 3] for is_color) from the walk’s current colour is at most chromatic_sigma², and moves to the mean position and the mean colour of those, both truncated to integers (ArrayFire’s __float2int_rz). The walk stops early when the position did not move, or moved by at most one pixel with a colour change of at most one. The result is the colour the walk ends with. The truncation makes the filter meant for images of 0..255: an image in 0..1 comes out all zero, like in ArrayFire.

A workgroup stages the pixels around its 16 x 16 tile (as far as workgroup memory allows, up to r) and the first step of every walk reads only from there; later steps move the window and read global memory through the cache, so any number of steps stays exact. The result is bit-identical to ArrayFire’s (both backends of ArrayFire agree here); the reciprocal of the pixel count, whose rounding decides the truncations, is corrected to the correctly rounded value on every backend.
